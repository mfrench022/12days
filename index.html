12 Days Sober

Michael French | September 28, 2026

I’ve been running from these thoughts for a long time. Drowning myself in liquor and cannabis smoke just to push them away. I stopped that twelve days ago, and I am overwhelmed with grief about the false promise of what my life could be, and I am struggling to stomach the fact that this is it.

In my 28 years on this earth, I’ve only watched it get worse. Our planet is dying. Our leaders are corrupt. Our children are becoming less literate, thoughtful, and caring. Our adults are becoming more hateful, spiteful, and bigoted. Our society is built around wealth instead of connection and encourages innovation at any expense.

Just this week, a war criminal spoke at the United Nations while peaceful protesters were arrested outside for opposing his ongoing genocide. This same week, it was revealed that the punishment for seven rapists who abused a young woman for hours on end was a reflective essay. And yet, I’ve been reading these kinds of stories my whole adult life, with each one being less surprising but more heartbreaking.

So this is it. This is the modern human experience. Work until death. Sift through the misery of everyday life in an oppressive, dying world, searching for crumbs of happiness until it’s finally over.

Each time my thoughts run down this path, I find myself feeling immensely guilty. I am incredibly privileged. I have parents who support me. I have a beautiful ten-year relationship with a loving partner. I’m educated. I’ve been given the freedom to explore hobbies and interests. Not to mention, my birth mother’s sacrifice of carrying me as a teenager and giving me up for adoption. All for me to still come to this point of despair in realizing that this is it.

It’s been almost eight years since the first fragments of realization began to form in my mind. I struggled to put the pieces together, so I numbed myself, using in excess. I was knowingly damaging my body, but I didn’t care. I wasn’t mad at the idea of shaving a bit of time off of my life. I’m not even really sure why I decided to stop. It certainly wasn’t out of some wholesome desire to take in the world without a safety net, nor was it really about self-improvement. But here I am.

I’m now trying to piece together where I go from here. And in doing so, I can’t help but reflect on how I got here.

Growing up as a closeted gay kid meant my aspiration was to survive. Senior year of high school, I was so deeply in denial about my sexuality that I was convinced I could get married, have kids, and live a normal heterosexual life, despite being completely incapable of feeling any sexual attraction towards a woman. This broken version of me was expected to then make a decision about what I wanted to do with my life and career, in the midst of cognitive dissonance.

So naturally, I made the wrong decision. I chose something that felt familiar and vaguely interesting. For a while, that was enough, until the looming threat of an unprofitable career became more and more real. I pivoted, got a corporate job in an adjacent field, and tried to set myself up for a lucrative career. After all, that’s what you’re supposed to do.

Three years in, and I was making six figures, drinking six beers a night, and smoking weed before morning meetings to take the edge off. I was burnt out and miserable, but so were my coworkers. So I went to grad school, just like every other mid-twenty-something in the midst of a career crisis. This time, I chose to study something more creative, something that I felt I would have more passion for. I thought I could tolerate the corporate world if I at least had some passion for my work.

I got through grad school at my lowest. I drank and drank. Sometimes even in class. Somehow, the overachieving closeted kid that still lives within me managed to mask the struggle. I was asked to speak on behalf of our class at graduation. I made honors. While high, drunk, and depressed.

Two years later, I have a master’s degree in graphic design, watching as my talented new peers lose passion for their craft. Most are underpaid, undervalued, overworked, and are being forced to adapt to new tools that we simply don’t want to use. That is, if they've even found a job. All for the sake of being in one of the increasingly rare corporate roles that comes with a little bit of creativity and humanity.

Jobs are scarce. Job hunting has become a brutal war of begging for a spot in an organization that ultimately wouldn’t care less if I live or die. All so that I can give them years of my life and receive nothing in return but the fickle promise of financial security. I’m tired of trying to prove that I have something to offer. And I’m yet again coming to terms with the fact that I may have made the wrong decision for my future.

As I write this essay, I am now twelve days sober, and the reality is setting in:

This is it.

I don’t know who I am.

I don’t know what I want.

Every decision I’ve made has been in service of following a path that I feel will please the people in my life or meet some hypothetical standard of what I am supposed to be doing.

I haven’t been able to see beyond that path. I don’t know what’s out there for me.

I know I’m not alone in these feelings. I even feel silly writing them down because I’m sure it’s all been said before, probably by someone in their twenties. And I might be stuck. I can’t change the world. But I am trying to learn to be okay with that uncertainty. Without it, how can I even begin to learn who I am and what I want?
